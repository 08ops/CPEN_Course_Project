1. Introduction and Scope

1.1 The problem

The Department of Computer Engineering intends to develop software that keeps five kinds of record: student personal information, student fees payments, course enrollment, the assignment of lecturers to courses, and the assignment of teaching assistants to lecturers. Project 1 asks for the relational database underneath that software, and for a Next.js 14 application with login, registration and a dashboard on top of it.

At present these five things live in different places — a spreadsheet of the class list, the Finance Office's own ledger, a paper registration form, and the Head of Department's teaching-allocation memo. Nothing reconciles them. The question "how much does this student still owe?" cannot be answered without opening two systems and trusting that both are current. The database built here exists to make that question answerable with one call.

2. Database Design

2.1 Why five schemas

The brief says to create schemas when needed. Twenty-three tables in a single public schema would work, but it would force prefix naming (fees_payment, academic_course) to keep related tables together visually, and it would make it impossible to grant an application account access to fees without also granting it access to everything else. The tables are therefore grouped by bounded context:

SchemaContentsRationale
coredepartment, programme, academic_year, semesterReference data every other schema points at
peopleperson, student, next_of_kin, lecturer, teaching_assistantFunctionality 1; a single source of truth for identity
academicscourse, course_prerequisite, course_offering, enrollment, lecturer_course_assignment, lecturer_ta_assignmentFunctionalities 3, 4, 5
financefee_structure, fee_item, student_bill, bill_line, paymentFunctionality 2; the schema with the tightest access requirements
appapp_user, user_session, audit_logAuthentication for the web app and the API — deliberately separate from people

Keeping app separate from people matters: a login is not a person. An administrator account has no personal record, and a person may exist in the department's records for years before ever creating a login. Conflating them would force nullable password columns onto the student table.

2.2 Entity-relationship design

The three design decisions worth defending

(a) A person supertype. Students, lecturers and teaching assistants all have a name, a date of birth, an e-mail address and a phone number. Repeating those columns in three tables would mean that a teaching assistant who is also a postgraduate student — which is the normal case — appears twice, with two e-mail addresses that can silently diverge. people.person holds the shared attributes once; student, lecturer and teaching_assistant are role tables each holding a UNIQUE foreign key to a person plus the columns unique to that role. In the sample data, TA/2025/001 and TA/2025/002 are the same people as MPhil students 24500112 and 24500198 — one person row each.

(b) course is separate from course_offering. CPEN 208 as a catalogue entry — its code, title and credit hours — is stable for years. CPEN 208 as taught in First Semester 2025/2026 has a lecturer, a venue, a capacity and a class list, all of which change every session. Enrolment and teaching assignment therefore reference the offering, not the course. Without this split, registering for CPEN 208 in two different years would be indistinguishable, and a lecturer could not be recorded as having taught it in one year but not the next.

(c) bill_line copies the fee items rather than referencing them. When a bill is generated, the amounts from the price list are copied onto the bill. If the department raises the Academic Facility User Fee next year, last year's bills must not silently change. The frozen copy is what makes the outstanding-fees figure historically truthful.

2.4 Keys, constraints and data integrity

Every table uses a surrogate primary key generated with GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY, and carries a natural unique key alongside it (student_number, staff_number, course_code, receipt_number). Surrogate keys keep foreign keys narrow and stable; the natural key is what humans quote and what the seed scripts join on, so the scripts contain no hard-coded ids.

The database enforces 36 foreign keys and 37 CHECK constraints. The rules that are genuinely interesting are the ones that stop the data from telling a story that cannot be true:

ConstraintWhat it prevents
student_hall_ckA resident student without a hall, or a commuter assigned to one
ta_student_link_ckA graduate/undergraduate TA with no student record, or an external TA with one
enrollment_dropped_ckA registration marked dropped with no date, or a date without the status
enrollment_uqRegistering for the same offering twice
student_bill_uqTwo bills for the same student in one academic year
student_bill_due_ckA due date that falls before the issue date
payment_amount_ckA zero or negative payment
lecturer_course_one_lead_uqTwo lead lecturers on one offering partial index
academic_year_one_current_uqTwo academic years flagged current partial index
next_of_kin_one_primary_uqTwo primary emergency contacts for one student partial index
A note on partial unique indexes

"At most one lead lecturer per offering" cannot be written as a UNIQUE constraint, because several co-lecturers are allowed on the same offering. PostgreSQL's partial index expresses it exactly: CREATE UNIQUE INDEX … ON lecturer_course_assignment (offering_id) WHERE teaching_role = 'lead_lecturer' AND is_active. The same technique enforces the single current academic year and the single primary next-of-kin.

The same trick solves a subtler problem in lecturer_ta_assignment. Its offering_id is nullable, because a TA may support a lecturer generally rather than on one named course. A plain UNIQUE(lecturer_id, ta_id, offering_id) would not stop duplicates, since SQL treats each NULL as distinct. Two partial indexes — one for rows with an offering, one for rows without — close that gap.

2.5 Indexing strategy

PostgreSQL already creates an index for every primary key and unique constraint, so only foreign-key columns and frequent filter or sort columns are indexed explicitly. Indexing everything would slow down writes for no benefit. Of the 97 indexes present, the ones that matter most carry the outstanding-fees calculation: 

-- The fees function only ever sums CONFIRMED money, so the index
-- stores only those rows: smaller, and it answers the query directly.
CREATE INDEX payment_confirmed_ix ON finance.payment (bill_id, amount)
                                  WHERE status = 'confirmed';

-- Live sessions only - expired rows are never looked up.
CREATE INDEX session_live_ix ON app.user_session (expires_at)
                             WHERE revoked_at IS NULL;

2.6 Triggers and maintained values

Four trigger functions keep derived data honest and enforce rules that a CHECK constraint cannot express, because they must look at other rows:

Trigger functionBehaviour
core.fn_set_updated_atStamps updated_at on every update, across all 18 tables that carry the column
finance.fn_refresh_bill_totalKeeps student_bill.total_amount equal to the sum of its bill lines
finance.fn_refresh_bill_statusRecalculates issued / part_paid / paid / overdue whenever a payment changes
academics.fn_validate_enrollmentRejects registration when the student is not active, the class is full, or registration is closed

student_bill.total_amount is a deliberate, maintained denormalisation. The outstanding-fees report reads it for every student on every call; recomputing SUM(bill_line.amount) each time would be wasteful. The trigger guarantees it can never drift from the sum of its lines, which is the only condition under which such a denormalisation is safe.

3. The Required Function — Outstanding Fees as a JSON Array

The requirement, verbatim

"Create a database function that will calculate the outstanding fees for each student in your database and return the output in json array."

3.1 Specification

finance.fn_outstanding_fees_json(
    p_academic_year_id INTEGER DEFAULT NULL,   -- NULL = every academic year
    p_programme_id     INTEGER DEFAULT NULL,   -- NULL = every programme
    p_only_indebted    BOOLEAN DEFAULT FALSE   -- TRUE = hide fully paid students
) RETURNS json

All three parameters are optional, so the no-argument call SELECT finance.fn_outstanding_fees_json(); satisfies the brief exactly while the same function still serves the filtered views the dashboard and the REST API need. The definition of the figure it returns is:

outstanding = Σ (amount billed) − Σ (payments with status confirmed)

Payments that are pending, reversed or failed are deliberately excluded. A cheque lodged but not yet cleared has not reduced what the student owes, and a reversed mobile-money transfer never did. This is the single most important business rule in the fees module, so the sample data contains one pending payment and one reversed payment purely so the exclusion can be demonstrated (§8).

3.2 Implementation

The function is written in three steps as a chain of CTEs. Abridged, with the reasoning in comments: 

WITH bill_payment AS (
    -- Step 1: collapse each bill to (billed, confirmed paid, last payment).
    -- The FILTER clause is what implements the confirmed-only rule.
    SELECT b.bill_id, b.student_id, b.total_amount, b.due_date, b.status,
           COALESCE(SUM(p.amount) FILTER (WHERE p.status = 'confirmed'), 0) AS paid,
           MAX(p.payment_date) FILTER (WHERE p.status = 'confirmed') AS last_payment_date
    FROM   finance.student_bill b
    LEFT   JOIN finance.payment p ON p.bill_id = b.bill_id
    WHERE  b.status <> 'cancelled'
      AND  (p_academic_year_id IS NULL OR b.academic_year_id = p_academic_year_id)
    GROUP  BY b.bill_id
),
student_totals AS (
    -- Step 2: roll the bills up to the student and nest the per-bill
    -- breakdown. The LEFT JOIN keeps students who have never been billed,
    -- so "each student in your database" really does mean each student.
    SELECT s.student_id, s.student_number, pr.first_name, pr.last_name, ...
           COALESCE(SUM(bp.total_amount), 0)                     AS total_billed,
           COALESCE(SUM(bp.paid), 0)                             AS total_paid,
           COALESCE(SUM(bp.total_amount), 0)
             - COALESCE(SUM(bp.paid), 0)                         AS outstanding_balance,
           COALESCE(json_agg(json_build_object(...))
                    FILTER (WHERE bp.bill_id IS NOT NULL), '[]'::json) AS bills
    FROM   people.student s
    JOIN   people.person    pr ON pr.person_id    = s.person_id
    JOIN   core.programme   pg ON pg.programme_id = s.programme_id
    LEFT   JOIN bill_payment bp ON bp.student_id  = s.student_id
    WHERE  (p_programme_id IS NULL OR s.programme_id = p_programme_id)
    GROUP  BY s.student_id, pr.person_id, pg.programme_id
)
-- Step 3: shape the array. COALESCE guarantees a valid empty array
-- rather than SQL NULL when no student matches the filters.
SELECT COALESCE(json_agg(json_build_object(
           'student_id', t.student_id, 'student_number', t.student_number,
           'full_name', ..., 'total_billed', ROUND(t.total_billed, 2),
           'total_paid', ROUND(t.total_paid, 2),
           'outstanding_balance', ROUND(t.outstanding_balance, 2),
           'payment_status', CASE WHEN t.total_billed = 0         THEN 'NOT BILLED'
                                  WHEN t.outstanding_balance <= 0 THEN 'FULLY PAID'
                                  WHEN t.total_paid = 0           THEN 'NO PAYMENT'
                                  ELSE 'PART PAID' END,
           'bills', t.bills)
         ORDER BY t.outstanding_balance DESC, t.student_number), '[]'::json)
INTO   v_result
FROM   student_totals t
WHERE  (NOT p_only_indebted OR t.outstanding_balance > 0);

Four details that are easy to get wrong

  • LEFT JOIN from student, not student_bill. An inner join would silently drop any student who has never been billed. The brief says each student, so an unbilled student must still appear — with a balance of zero and the status NOT BILLED.
  • FILTER rather than a WHERE clause. Filtering the join to confirmed payments in WHERE would also remove bills that have only unconfirmed payments, hiding exactly the debt the report exists to surface.
  • COALESCE(json_agg(...), '[]'). With no matching rows, json_agg returns SQL NULL, not an empty array. A client doing data.length would then fail. The COALESCE guarantees the return value is always a JSON array — which is what the brief asks for.
  • json_agg(... ) FILTER (WHERE bp.bill_id IS NOT NULL). Without the filter, an unbilled student's nested bills array would contain one object full of nulls instead of being empty.

3.4 Proof of correctness

Asserting that a function is correct is not the same as showing it. Section 7d of 07_verification.sql recomputes every balance a second time, independently, in plain SQL that shares no code with the function, and compares the two: 

WITH from_function AS (
    SELECT (e->>'student_id')::INT, (e->>'outstanding_balance')::NUMERIC
    FROM   json_array_elements(finance.fn_outstanding_fees_json()) AS e
),
from_plain_sql AS (
    SELECT s.student_id,
           COALESCE(SUM(b.total_amount), 0)
         - COALESCE(SUM((SELECT SUM(p.amount) FROM finance.payment p
                         WHERE p.bill_id = b.bill_id AND p.status = 'confirmed')), 0)
    FROM   people.student s
    LEFT   JOIN finance.student_bill b ON b.student_id = s.student_id
                                      AND b.status <> 'cancelled'
    GROUP  BY s.student_id
)
SELECT COUNT(*) AS students_compared,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE fn_outstanding <> sql_outstanding) AS mismatches,
       SUM(ABS(fn_outstanding - sql_outstanding)) AS total_difference
FROM   from_function JOIN from_plain_sql USING (student_id);
 students_compared | mismatches | total_difference
-------------------+------------+------------------
                36 |          0 |             0.00

5.2 Privacy assumption

Stated explicitly

Apart from the author's own record (22128981), all personal details in the sample — names, dates of birth, Ghana Card numbers, phone numbers, e-mail addresses and fee balances — are synthetic stand-ins for real classmates and staff. They are realistic in shape, so that every constraint, trigger and function is genuinely exercised, but they identify nobody. Publishing real classmates' dates of birth, national identity numbers and outstanding fee balances to a public repository would not be defensible, and the brief's instruction to "make all necessary assumptions" is taken to cover this. The lecturers' names are likewise invented and are not intended to correspond to any member of staff.

6. The Next.js 14 Application

6.1 Architecture

Question 2 asks for a Next.js 14 application with login, register and a dashboard. It is built on the App Router with TypeScript in strict mode and Tailwind CSS, and it talks to PostgreSQL directly through a pooled pg client. No ORM is used — the queries already exist in the database as functions, and an ORM would add a translation layer over SQL that is deliberately hand-written.

6.2 Register, login and sessions

Registration validates the form with zod, hashes the password with bcrypt, and calls app.fn_register_user(...). If the user supplies an index number, the new login is linked to the existing people.person row, so their real fees and courses appear the moment they sign in. This was verified live: registering the account efua.lamptey against index number 22129055 produced a session whose dashboard immediately showed that student's true outstanding balance of GH₵1,788.00 and her seven registered courses.

Login accepts a username, an index number or an e-mail address. An unknown user and a wrong password return the identical message, so the form cannot be used to discover which accounts exist; the code even runs a dummy bcrypt comparison in the unknown-user branch so the two paths take comparable time. Five consecutive failures lock the account for fifteen minutes.

Sessions are the part most often done badly. The browser receives a 32-byte random token in an httpOnly cookie; the database stores only its SHA-256 hash in app.user_session. Anyone who obtains a database dump therefore cannot replay a live session. Sessions expire after seven days and can be revoked server-side, which a stateless JWT could not offer.

Why middleware alone is not the security boundary

src/middleware.ts runs on the Edge runtime, which cannot open a TCP connection to PostgreSQL. It can therefore only check that a session cookie is present. The authoritative check is getCurrentUser() in the dashboard layout, which validates the token against app.user_session on every request. A forged cookie passes middleware and is then rejected by the layout, which redirects to /login. Treating middleware as the security boundary — a common mistake — would leave the dashboard open to anyone who set a cookie by hand.

11. Challenges, Limitations and Future Work

Challenges met during development

  • Bills dated relative to "today". The first run of the seed script failed on student_bill_due_ck: issued_on defaulted to the current date, which fell after the October 2025 due date being loaded. Rather than move the due date, the fix was conceptual — a bill belongs to the academic year it charges for, so fn_generate_student_bill now defaults issued_on to the first day of that year. The scripts are now reproducible however long after the session they are run.
  • A negative balance. After adding an explicit instalment plan for the author's record, the dashboard showed a balance of minus GH₵1,412.00. The explicit payments were being added on top of the pattern-generated ones rather than replacing them. The seed now excludes that student from the generated pattern, and the verification suite gained a check that no student's balance is ever negative.
  • CREATE OR REPLACE does not replace a signature. Adding a fourth parameter to fn_generate_student_bill created a second overload instead of replacing the function, and the next call failed as ambiguous. The script now drops the old signature explicitly, so it remains re-runnable against a database built by an earlier version.
  • A 'use server' file may export only async functions. Exporting the shared form-state object from actions.ts compiled cleanly but crashed at runtime on the first login. The type and constant moved to lib/formState.ts.

Limitations

  • Grades are stored but there is no transcript or CGPA recalculation routine; student.cgpa is set from the sample data rather than derived.
  • Prerequisites are recorded but not enforced at registration; the trigger checks capacity, status and the registration window only.
  • There is no timetable-clash detection — a student may register for two courses meeting at the same hour.
  • Fee payment is recorded, not taken: there is no payment-gateway integration.
  • Row-level security is not enabled; access control lives in the application and API layers.

Future work

  • Enforce prerequisites and timetable clashes inside fn_enroll_student, where every client would inherit them.
  • Add PostgreSQL row-level security so the database itself restricts a student to their own rows.
  • Partition finance.payment and app.audit_log by year once they grow.
  • Materialise the outstanding-fees report if the student population reaches a scale where per-request computation is measurable.

12. Conclusion

Every item in the Project 1 brief has been delivered: a PostgreSQL database named cpen208_ceds with five purposeful schemas, 23 tables covering all five required functionalities, insert scripts that populate every one of them with the CPEN 208 class, and — the headline requirement — finance.fn_outstanding_fees_json(), which calculates the outstanding fees for each student and returns them as a JSON array. Alongside it, a Next.js 14 application provides login, registration and a role-aware dashboard, and four restore-tested backups accompany the scripts.

The decision that shaped everything else was to put the business rules in the database rather than in application code. "Outstanding fees" is defined once, in SQL, and is therefore the same number whether it is read from psql, rendered on the dashboard, or — as Project 2 goes on to show — returned over HTTP. Correctness was not asserted but demonstrated: the function's output is cross-checked against independently written SQL for all 36 students, with zero mismatches, and the exclusion of pending and reversed payments is proved against data planted specifically to test it.
